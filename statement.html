Artist's Statement

On painting
and the problem of the surface

I have been making paintings for most of my life, and the thing that still interests me — the thing that keeps me in the studio — is a question I could not have put into words when I started: what is actually happening between a person and a flat surface covered in coloured matter? It sounds almost too simple to be worth asking. But I find it inexhaustible.

My earlier work was figurative. I spent a long time learning to look carefully — at bodies, at light, at the particular way a hand holds itself when a person is thinking. That discipline still lives in everything I do. You do not stop caring about weight and presence just because you stop painting recognisable things.

At some point the figure began to feel like a solution I already knew. I was more interested in the questions that didn't have answers yet — how colour creates space that isn't there, how a mark can feel urgent or resigned depending on what surrounds it, how the eye moves and why. The abstract work came out of following those questions rather than illustrating what I already understood.

"The painting is not a record of what I saw or felt. It is the thing itself — whatever that turns out to be."

I don't think of the two phases of my work as separate. The realist paintings were always, at some level, about paint. The abstract ones are always, at some level, about the body — its weight, its temperature, the way it occupies space. The picture plane is where those two things meet, and I am still not sure what happens there. That uncertainty is what keeps me working.

I work in Valencia, where I have lived for a number of years now, after a long time in the United States and before that Hungary, where I was born. My family left in 1956, during the Revolution — I was nine years old. Each of those places is somewhere in the paintings, though I would be hard pressed to tell you exactly where.
